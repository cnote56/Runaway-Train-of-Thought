Chapter One

The earth was intent on killing its children.

The Sun was its willing accomplice.

The bitter driving wild and cold pressed through the frozen, cracked
rubber seals between the metal hull plates and internal electrical ducts
and conduits. The cold was unrelenting these cycles, one after another,
relentless.

And there was no avoiding it anymore.

Underwater earthquakes, one after another triggered around the globe.
Then bigger and bigger and stronger than ever recorded. Ever before.

Everywhere around the world.

Every seismometer maxed out on its sensors.

Suddenly, the skies flashed with reflections of the sun's radiation,
causing a worldwide Arora Borealis effect that bathed the worth in
lethal doses of a buffet of solar radiation. This radiation bath lasted
three weeks.

During this hellish bath, anything on the surface caught in the initial
blast of these solar storms found itself slowly dying from radiation
poisoning. The birds abandoned the upper atmosphere only to be frozen to
death where they weren’t cooked alive by the solar rays; exposed spots
of the earth surfaced and faced terrifying radiation scarring whole
swaths of Europe.

The lowest layers of the atmosphere were now under a thick cloud of ash
and cinder from the erupting volcanoes on every continent.

The earth's magnetic field had slowed down by the neutrino storms
carried by the radiation waves from the sun’s tantrum. The neutrinos
usually pass through with little to no observable effect on everything
known to man.

Still, the rush and sheer amount of neutrinos caused the earth's
rotation to grind slower fractionally at first but then slower and
slower. Slowing it down to fractions of its average speed. Then the
neutrinos waves would relent, and the magnetic pull of the core would
speed up, only to be battered back by the waves of the neutrino sunami
wrecking the rotation of the earth’s core. The slow grind and back and
forth crushed all complaints against the dangers of climate change.

And to the survivors go the spoils of a brutalized world. Fifty years of
driving cold blizzard-like conditions coupled with brief spring-like
thaws and then incredibly robust and violent summer months with shifts
in the weather so slight it makes 30-degree changes in a single day seem
like a mundane existence.

For those that survived the sudden freezing winters and encroached on
new ice shelves and freezing of the Artic and SAubartic seas and
portions of the Indian, Pacific, and Atlantic oceans.

from the snow and cold, Brady sits in his survival pod command center
chair wrapped in a thick blanket around his coated shoulders with his
fingerless gloves and fleshy tips poking out of mounds of blankets and
fabric.

The cold keeps Brady from finding comfort, and he turns to the glow of
his station terminal and loads an old news report from the very
beginning of what has been dubbed now as The Great Freeze.

The bitter driving wild and cold pressed in through the broken rubber
seals between the metal hull plates and internal electrical ducts and
conduits. The cold was unrelenting these cycles, all too quickly too.

And there was no avoiding it anymore.

An underwater earthquake. The biggest one ever recorded. Ever.
Everywhere around the world. Every seismometer maxed out on its sensors;
the earth shook itself to its core a moment later.

Inside from the snow and cold, Brady sits in his office chair wrapped in
a thick blanket around his coated shoulders with his fingerless gloves
and fleshy tips poking out of mounds of blankets and fabric.

The archives had all the data; it happened so plainly and blindly; the
video plays.

“Scientists from Harvard and Yale hypothesize a solution to global
warming may be as simple as turning down the heat. A highly respected
group of meteorologists and planetary scientists have all agreed to
explore the concept of controlling the nuclear mechanics of the sun…”

“Why are you watching those history files?” Janet, a geologist by study
and wife to Brady by choice, entered the room with a tray of ice
samples. Her thick coat zipped halfway up.

Brady looked up at Janet and sighed. "Just trying to find some hope," he
said, gesturing to the computer screen. "Remember when people used to
think that we could fix things with just a flick of a switch? That we
could control the sun? It seems so naive now."

Janet nodded, setting the tray of ice samples on the table beside Brady.
"It does," she said. "But we can't give up hope. We must keep fighting
and trying to find solutions, no matter how bleak things seem."

Brady nodded, taking a sip from the cup of warm tea that Janet had
brought for him. "You're right," he said. "We can't give up. We must
keep pushing forward, no matter how hard it gets."

They both sat silently, listening to the howling winds outside. Brady
couldn't help but think of the world that was, the world that could have
been.

Brady chuckled at Janet's comment, taking another sip of his tea. "You
have a point," he said. "But it feels like we're running out of options.
Every day the cold gets worse, the storms get stronger, and the ice gets
thicker. We’re not built for these conditions.”

Janet nodded, understanding the weight of Brady's words. "I know," she
said. "But we can't give up hope. We must keep looking for solutions and
trying to find a way to reverse the damage that's been done. We owe it
to ourselves and to the world we live in."

Brady bristled at Janet, grateful for her unwavering optimism in the
face of such despair but sick of being so damned impotent to stop it and
save their lives.

“I don’t know,” Brady sighed and swiped the video file away from his
screen. “I guess I keep thinking if we just go back as far as we can, we
might find something in their failures, before they were successful, on
how we might be able to reverse what they did.”

“Not a bad theory, but old news reports aren’t going to get you anywhere
worth going. Back then, they barely read the first three letters of a
word before deciding whether to finish reading the whole word. Elections
were lost because people did not bother to read the whole name. Do you
think their news reports were any more readable?”

Brady grumbled and fished through a basket of data plugs.

“You’ve already got the personal notes of half the original team.
Nothing in there, right?” Jan turned away and rubbed her hands together,
trying to warm them as he prepared her ice samples for cataloging.

Suddenly the power failed; everything blacked out.

“Wait for the generators,” Brady said and fluffed his coat, trying to
keep his warmth from dissipating.

“Yeah, so this is how it all will end up?” Jan asked.

The two of them had already consolidated their folds of coats and cloaks
into sandwich layers of sorts, one blanket wrapped into another wrapped
over into another like the folds of a rose.

“Yeah, with a whimper, I guess.” Brady did the mental math as he spoke.
“We’ve got roughly 24 hours worth of stasis in the habitat. With no
seals failing or doors flying open and no other major breaches to the
outer skin, we’ll slowly get colder over the next day or so.”

“And then,” Jan asked.

“Then we get to take the last of this and settle for a long winter's
nap.” Brady produced a sealed bottle of whiskey from under his folds of
blankets and coats.

“You’re solution is to drink ourselves to death?” Jan guffawed.

“Well, yeah.” Brady said plainly, “I've done the numbers, Jan. There is
not much else to do. We won't be able to head anything that we have left
that could be eaten. The generators are out. Kaput. Finito. The
temperature is too low for the damn combustion cores even to ignite. So,
we might as well have a drink and enjoy the most boring show on earth.”

And it would be the most boring show on earth. This was the last gasp of
the human race. Sipping whiskey and waiting for the cold to overtake
them.

The idea struck Brady like the quickening strikes an embryo in utero.

“What if there is a place we can live? Without the Radiation and the
Ice? There must be some part of the ocean where we can sink a habitat,
tap the currents for electricity, and desalinate as needed for water. We
grow algae and …. He paused, his mind now fevered with racing thoughts.”

Janet's eyes widened as she saw the excitement in Brady's expression.
"What are you thinking?" she asked, curious.

"I don't know yet," Brady said, his mind racing with possibilities. "But
what if we could build a self-sustaining habitat in the ocean, free from
the ravages of radiation and ice? We could grow food, generate power,
and access unlimited ocean water. It could be a new way of living, a new
hope for humanity."

Fueled by the fire of innovation, Brady swept himself into his computer
station, throwing his ideas into archives and databases of all the
available repositories worldwide. His credentials from the National
World Weather Agency allowed him to hurdle initial interagency red tape
left over by ancient societies and suddenly declassify troves of data
and projects focused on any research or theorem, prototypes, or DARPA
projects focused on underwater survivability.

He stared at the flickering computer screen in front of him, the dim
glow casting eerie shadows around the room. He was one of the lucky ones
with access to one of the last remaining functioning computers in the
world. It was his job to keep track of the remaining resources and
coordinate with the other scattered groups of survivors.

But as he looked at the dwindling numbers, he felt a sense of
hopelessness wash over him. The world he once knew was gone, and he
couldn't help but wonder if anything would ever be the same again. The
once-lush forests were now barren wastelands, and the oceans had turned
into acidic pools filled with garbage and pollution. The wildlife that
once roamed the land was now extinct, replaced by mutated creatures that
barely resembled their ancestors.

Brady sighed, feeling the weight of the world on his shoulders. He knew
he couldn't give up, even though the situation seemed bleak. There was
still a chance, a glimmer of hope that they might make things right
again.

He closed his eyes, imagining a world where the trees were tall and
green, the oceans clear and blue, and the wildlife thrived. It was a
world he hoped to see one day again, but for now, he had to focus on the
task at hand.

As he opened his eyes and turned back to the computer screen, he
promised himself and the rest of the survivors that he would do
everything he could to make a better future for them and the world. It
may be an uphill battle, but he was ready to fight for it, no matter how
long it took.

And then the laboratory screens went blank, and the generators tapped
themselves. On, and the monitors slowly clicked back on.

Brady’s screen remained blank.

Brady's mind raced with possibilities as he and Janet mumbled the idea
between themselves; a self-sustaining habitat in the ocean.

Suddenly, a notification appeared on his terminal screen, indicating an
incoming transmission.

Brady hesitated for a moment, wondering who could be trying to contact
him in the middle of the frozen wasteland. But curiosity got the better
of him, and he opened the message.

To his surprise, the message was from a secret survival colony of
elites, and military leaders hidden deep beneath the ocean's surface.
The message was an invitation to visit the colony and share their
knowledge and resources to help save humanity from the ravages of
climate change.

Brady was stunned. He had no idea that such a colony existed, let alone
that they would be willing to help him and Janet with their concept. But
he knew this could be a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity to make a real
difference in the world.

With Janet's encouragement, Brady agreed to visit the colony and learn
more about their survival plans. They packed their bags and embarked on
a perilous journey across the frozen wasteland through blizzards and ice
storms until they reached the hidden entrance to the colony deep beneath
the waves.

What they found there was beyond anything they could have imagined.
