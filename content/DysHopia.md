The Inyo Mountains

They were named in honor of the Paiute native American indigenous tribe,
the Inyo Mountains specifically.

The Panamint band of Paiute-Shoshone people who inhabited the area since
humanity's prehistoric roots. When the first white settlers asked the
local Paiute for the name of the mountains, they responded that the land
belonged to the Shoshone tribe and their chief, Inyo, who was the
headman of the Panamint band of Paiute-Shoshone people at the time.
Thus, the white man’s continued demand for order and ownership of all
things drew maps and claims to the land as the native Paiute Tribe
hunted, fished, lived, and caretook receded.

The wilderness area is known for its rich mining legacy, with remnants

Cerro Gordo and the History of Mankind

In 1865, silver was discovered by Pablo Flores, a Mexican prospector who
began mining and smelting operations near the summit of Buena Vista
Peak. Tales of the silver at Cerro Gordo quickly spread, attracting many
prospectors to the area by 1867. The surface ores were rich and less
complex than those in other districts, allowing miners to extract
significant quantities of silver with hand labor and crude roasting
ovens made of adobe.

By 1869, Americans had taken over the property and turned Cerro Gordo
into California's largest producer of silver and lead. The mines yielded
high-grade silver and lead ores, with assays as high as $300 per ton.
Cerro Gordo increased, with hundreds of dwellings and teams of mules
traveling between Cerro Gordo and Los Angeles for transportation. By the
1880s, the mining of silver and lead peaked, followed by a second mining
boom in the 1910s, which focused on zinc production. Two smelters were
erected at Cerro Gordo, and the mines continued to be productive. Over
time, the mining operations at Cerro Gordo declined, and the town became
a California ghost town. Then, it turned to private hands and a spry
preservationist community devoted to preserving the history of the
silver mine that had developed in the early 21st century. Still, sadly,
its presence has vanished - scrubbed from the earth by nuclear fallout.

Above the earth, as the final war flamed below, the last toehold of
humanity hung in orbit. Continuously occupied since 2030, the Atlas
Space Station orbits the Earth at an altitude of about 250 miles (400
kilometers). This altitude strikes a balance between being close enough
to Earth for resupply missions and being high enough to minimize
atmospheric drag. Therefore, a permanent orbital base must also be
located in a similar low Earth orbit.

#### Energy Requirements

A space station requires a constant supply of energy to counteract the
effects of atmospheric drag and maintain its altitude. This energy is
primarily used for propulsion and maintaining life support systems, such
as providing power for lighting, temperature control, and life support
equipment.

Energy Sources

While nuclear fission has been used in the past to provide power for
space missions, alternative energy sources can be considered for a
permanent orbital base. Solar power is a viable option, as it has been
successfully used on the ISS. The ISS relies on large solar arrays to
generate electricity from sunlight, which is then stored in batteries
during orbital night when the station is in Earth's shadow.

Solar power has several advantages, including its abundance in space and
its sustainability. It is a clean and renewable energy source harnessed
in the space environment. Additionally, advancements in solar panel
technology have made them more efficient and lightweight, making them
suitable for space applications.

International Peace Organization and a Lifeboat Structure

A permanent orbital base would need to be located in a low Earth orbit,
have a constant energy supply to stay in orbit, and consider alternative
energy sources such as solar power.
