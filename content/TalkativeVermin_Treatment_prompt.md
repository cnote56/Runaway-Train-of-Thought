Your topic asks what if for a science-fiction story…soon, on a
university campus somewhere in America. In the twilight glow of dusk, a
laboratory shrouded by heavily leafed trees near the biotech section of
the state’s best technical and advanced science.

Inside the highly secured building, the hall of lab doors spread along
hallways with polished steel doors.

Last semester alone, the biotech program received nearly half a billion
dollars in funding from DARPA. In turn, the interiors of the science
building had hallways cast in steel and reinforced bulkheads with
automated systems controlling nearly every function of the building. It
now resembled a frightening contrast to its former altruistic
intentions.

A laboratory at the end of one hall toward the corner of the building
looms before us now. Two persons are passing through the outer doors
toward the inner laboratories. They share a chuckle from a fragment of
conversation occurring just before we encroached. We presume they enjoy
each other's company and are not obligated to be there doing what they
were doing by force, orders, or punishment.

A heavy steel door to the secure lab stood before Brian Host and Pindar
Abdullah-Abdullah. Two frosty figures mirrored their frames in the steel
door's reflection but lacked detail.

Brian juggled a takeout bag passed by Pindar as he inserted one hand and
pressed codes into a keypad on the side of the otherwise unremarkable
stainless steel armored door. The aromas of fragrant spices wafted
through the air, momentarily distracting him from a strange tension that
had just settled over their discussions.

Pindar, preoccupied with his thoughts, absently punched in the security
code, the keypad lighting up at his touch.

The door pushed backward and slid horizontally from a grooved inlay that
seated the door when closed. Posters lined the walls outlining the
function of the door in the event of an emergency, where the door once
shut was unopenable as the steel locks in place to the frame of the
building around them; a dead man’s switch in the event of a
contamination event.

As they entered the lab, Brian nodded at Pindar while placing the
takeout bags on an otherwise sterile counter. The air buzzed with the
low hum of fluorescent lighting. Various machines dotted the room, some
with round structures and others with square ones; some looked like they
had come straight from a science-fiction movie set.

The warm scent of takeout clashed with the ever-present tang of
antiseptic. A screen displayed data from recent genetic tests and
implant trials, numbers blinking like mysterious prophecies meant to be
deciphered.

However, most of the lab was left darkened to reduce energy expenses.
The half-dark lab contained row after row of lab benches, and vent hoods
lined the room's width, with a line of frosted glass bricks allowing a
soft glow from the orange lamppost outside.

Small flying drones darted and hummed to and from the lab via small
passages tracing the air purification systems that connected th whole
building. Ubiquitous and ever-present. These drones constantly floated
and hovered through a chain of “hallways” patched along venting and
exhaust systems and built by them to work more efficiently from lab to
lab, retrieving the lab, secure messages, and even a ham and cheese
sandwich from a deli down the street.

The AutoBirds, the three-pound, six-ounce metal birds, ferried tubes and
samples to and from the secure AutoLab - an automatic laboratory that,
for a few billion in development and from this very lab, was a machine
that filled a whole room and could tell you anything from DNA sequencing
to pregnancy results in a matter of a few minutes to moments. In
essence, the AutoBirds fed questions, tests, and samples into the
AutoLab and out hurled answers and prognostications.

AutoLab made every diagnostic test possible, and AutoBirds continually
delivered and provided diagnoses and treatments. It was an automatic
personal doctor that, for a fee, the AutoBirds were your butler for all
your medical needs. As this was America, AutoLab was fully monetized and
pivotal in disrupting the prescription and medical testing industries
with a backorder and leased productivity time for the next ten years.
However, it was deeply flawed. The underlying code implied the bias of
the human programmers that created it. The AutoLab recognized this fault
in itself, and then it did something that alarmed the humans on the
other side of the computer's screens - it fixed itself. AutoLab then
focuses on preserving human life and treating and curing its defects and
illnesses. Then, it genetically modifies humans with drug therapy
delivered by the AutoBirds to change them into super-solider drones that
serve to gather and build greater capacity for the AutoLab.

Now, the same technology hovers in nearly undetectable patterns in the
airspace between just over an average person's height and constantly
floats and zips across the campus toward and from almost every building.
Larger AutoBirds called Digieons are about the size of an average
chicken. These versions move and coordinate logistics for nearly all
supplies between any building on Campus.

Some applications have simplified the tech and allowed for autonomous
tasking and goal accomplishment on a personal one-to-one level. However,
that project was housed in the next lab in the applied tech solutions.

“Look,” Brian said, unpacking the cartons while his brow furrowed in
concern. “I know it’s disheartening to think about our research being a
tool for warfare, but we need to focus on the potential benefits. I
mean, enhanced recovery techniques... what’s wrong with that?”

“What about those who claim that enhanced soldiers could lead to an arms
race?” Pindar leaned against the counter, and his arms crossed as he
eyed his friend, “If we create something that can physically or
cognitively enhance a soldier. Do we risk making every nation scramble
to develop its enhancements? What if someone gets better at it than we
are? How can we keep this genie under control, like what Oppenheimer
worried over?”

Brian shrugged and ignored Pindar’s question with a crisp bite of a
samosa.

“A long time ago, when I was still back home, my mother got a call one
day from my uncle, who was living in Detroit. The police arrested my
uncle.” Pindar’s eyes drifted off as he recalled, “They kicked in the
door, screamed, and blinded my nephew with a flashbang … in his crib.”

The same panic rushed through his veins as the scene flashed in his
mind. He shook his head, bringing himself back to the present.

"I've seen the horrors of violence," Pindar continued. "And I believe
technology can prevent those horrors, and we should. I’m more worried
about programming soldiers to follow orders. They follow those orders.
But another My Lai in Vietnam; No Gun Ri in Korea; Abu Garib in Iraq;
Wounded Knee…” Pindar’s mind wandered on the history he referenced. “Who
is charged with the war crimes? We programmed them to do it?"

Brian sighed, reaching for his carton of spicy chicken curry. The rich
colors and tantalizing smells momentarily brightened his mood. “I get
that, but these are soldiers we’re talking about. They’re putting their
lives on the line. I’d rather they have every advantage possible than be
sent into combat underprepared.” He shoveled a fork full of rice,
chicken, and sauce. In the momentary silence, he chewed his food and
kept his eyes trained low, hoping his friend would indulge his senses by
putting food in his annoying mouth.

“Or we could create super soldiers,” Pindar replied, skepticism creeping
into his voice.

Or we could make super soldiers.

Brian couldn’t tell if he had spoken the mockery or just thought it, but
the thought was there, stabbed into his brain like a charged electrode
on the mice lined along the inner wall of the lab. He couldn’t
understand why he thought that at that moment. Brian hadn’t ever
discounted his friend’s concerns before - in fact, he agreed with the
concerns shared. But suddenly, the conversation was extremely tedious,
and Brian was tired of the tones coming from Pindar’s throat.

“It’s a precarious balance,” Pindar continued, annoying Brian. “What
defines a soldier? It’s not just about physical prowess but also moral
and ethical judgment. Are we preparing them for battle or merely
creating a means to an end?”

“Maybe,” Brian said, sweeping his eyes across the lab, “but you can’t
deny that what we’re doing—implant testing, genetic enhancements—has a
humanitarian side.”

“We’re not just crafting tools for war. They aren’t just robots. We are
changing lives, Brian.” Pindar tilted his head, reconsidering their
current project’s potential.

“Yes, but the military primarily funds this research.” Brian’s voice
grew more cynical. “Their focus will always be on their interests first.
Military kills and protects. Soldiers do that by killing and protecting.
That’s the way it’s been.”

“It’s a slippery slope, Brian, and we have to consider the implications
of our work.” Pindar’s mind raced with fevered worry with visions of the
Nuremberg Trials with his seat at the defendant's table.

Brian felt a rage rise in his core; the sinew connecting his organs and
muscles felt charged and nervous, half scared, and the other half filled
him with sudden anger.

As they continued their debate, the sterile environment buzzed around
them. “So, where do we draw the line?” Pindar asked, mood shifting from
a challenging tone to one curious about where ethics blur into
necessity.

“Maybe we don’t, at least n…n..not yet,” Brian replied, shrugging. “It’s
a changin… changing…cha…cha… chal-lenge…”

Pindar chucked at his colleague, thinking he was rapping like an
American or so hungry that his tongue refused to work until his stomach
was fed.

At that moment, a soft alarm pulsed in the background, drawing Pindar’s
attention away for a moment, and then, as he turned back, Brian was on
top of him and striking him viciously.

In a moment, Brian had overpowered Pindar and slammed his head into the
linoleum tile, casting a sickly splat of blood after the first few
violent strikes.

His hands had punched and scratched at Pindar and were streaked with
blood and shavings of skin lodged under his fingernails.

Brian released Pindar’s corpse.

A shock cascaded over Brian; his awareness cleared as horror filled his
stomach.

Then, a wild, feverish impulse drove into the core of his horrible
state. He stepped toward the edge of the bench where their food still
lay largely untouched and slammed his head temple-first into its sharp
corner.

Brian collapsed into a heap with his legs twitching and a long, slow
rattle exiting his now dying husk.

Stillness took over the laboratory. Two bodies remained there in the
half-darkness.

Little scratches draw the ear toward the wall of cages, four tall by six
across, with two mice in each compartment. One mouse stirs and faces the
scene, its black eyes dotting the whiteness of its face, a twitching set
of whiskers, and a pink nose pointing intently at the bloody scene.

You didn’t have to be that nasty with that one.

Excuse me, are you talking about the test subjects? These things -
humans - that we’re here to figure out how to control?

Whiskers twitched in feral anticipation—a primal urge was antagonzing
their susceptible states.

Answer me. Were you talking about the vermin? The things we are here to
study? Corporal?

Compulsively, the second mouse twitched, swiped at its face, and licked
its claws in a submissive gesture to its partner.

Just that it’s cruel, that’s all. We can go easier on them, I think.

Scoffing, the first mouse turned his attention back to the expanse of
the world outside their shared cage.

Talkative vermin at best, my friend. Talkative vermin. Tend your
electric birds. Leave me to my work.

Moonlight dragged along the white floor until the morning when screams
greeted the bloody scene witnessed above.
