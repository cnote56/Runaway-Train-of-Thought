Hell’s kitchen

The ashes floated slowly to the floor.  His eyes followed the simple
predestined downward motion until the ashes found their place. The
Manufactured air filled his tobacco stained lungs.  He coughed and hacked a
clear breathing path for the unnatural air around him.  The walls seemed
grayer to him to day, and his bed seemed a little harder.  As the dirty
yellow sun climbed slowly above the horizon, he slowly stood to attention
and found the familiar path to his corner of the street.  From his
newsstand, his perch, he saw all walks of society’s lives.  From the
poorest of beggars crying out for the few pennies that fall from the loose
pockets of the richest men to the bugs crawling in the gutters below their
feet.
The titles of his newspapers and his magazines never seemed to change.
There seemed no new news to be made.  What ever that could be done has been
done and whatever concerned the men who have the power to change the things
in need of changing, has not concerned them. There seems no disease deadly
enough to merit the concern of the ones who have control.  This segregation
of which I speak is necessary and is not considered segregation, its
current word is natural order. The natural order is handled in two ways.
One is what ever your father was you should become that too. Whether he was
a great poet or one of the lowliest shopkeepers the father’s life is taken
over by the son.  It is not uncommon for fathers to end their lives to
allow their sons the chance to have a good life without worrying about the
futures outcomes.  Disobedience of this law is not permitted.  There is a
second choice to the first; there are always two choices here.  The choice
or direction to be taken is a test for aptitude.  Although many do not
accept this challenge for there have been few to become greater than there
father.
Our Newspaperman is one of the few that have obtained something greater
than what his father was.  The Newspaperman’s father was not of nobility
nor of the poorest quality his father was not of a certain inherited
respect nor was he spat upon by the highest of moneymen.  He was born into
the Second best caste he was of the workers.  His father didn’t break any
of the sacred caste laws. But our newspaperman does not intend to supply
his fellow citizens with old news and uninteresting periodicals.
