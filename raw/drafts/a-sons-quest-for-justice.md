      The air stuck to your skin like it was made of dirt and honey. There
was a brown tint to the world on that Saturday. I was waiting outside the
barbershop cause my dad had a meeting with the union leaders and I wasn’t
supposed to be there-hearing what my daddies employers had planned for him
and his union brothers. I was looking at the girls walk by in the
sundresses when my father stepped out of the barbershop and tapped me on
the shoulder.

      “You gonna just stare at ‘em or you gonna one day get the balls to
talk to one of ‘em.” Daddy’s southern accent was thick after having to
politic with the boys all afternoon, “Come-on we gotta get on home before
it gets to late. You mother gonna start worrying.”


      The truck bounced it way across the road as old country tunes squeaked
out the of paper speakers. We called the truck old blacky even though it
was currently painted blue. I watched the sun dip below the tops of the
tall pines trees as we found our way through the back roads of North
Carolina hill country. Daddy would belt out the words of the country songs
coming out of the radio like he had heard them a thousand times before. His
thick baritone voice sounded over the creaks, squeaks, and groans of the
old blacky. His forearm hung out of the window like a side of ham. It was
as thick as a cow’s leg and the hair on it fluttered in the wind.  We
passed the country store where I would sometimes get a pack of baseball
cards- with a single thick stick of hard gum that seemed impossible to chew
until you got two or three bites into it.


      “Ah hell!” Daddy snapped his fingers and exclaimed,” We forgot the
milk.”


      He slowed old blacky and whipped a quick u-turn. “Here take a couple
of dollars and run in there and get a gallon.” He whipped out his black
billfold and pulled two pale green dollar bills out and thrust them at me.


      The gravel crunched under my feet as I ran full speed for the wooden
door of the store. The bell rung and the cold air of air conditioning feel
good on my senses. I opened the milk cooler. I tugged on a heavy gallon of
the cold milk and handed over the two pale dollar bills. Sixty-eight cents
in change was in my hand has daddy leaned over to open the door of the
truck. The milk bounced onto the seat of the truck and I held out my hand
with the Sixty-eight cents in change.


      “Keep it, save it up so you can get a pack a cards next time.”


      I plunged the money in my left front pocket with glee and muttered,
“thank you daddy.”  I held on to milk when we turned off onto our dirt
road.


      The dust sprayed out from the sides of the tires as I heard the crunch
and rustle of the dirt and gravel spread under the black rubber of the
tires. We were coming over the hill when I saw grampa bring the tractor in
from the back forty acres he waved as daddy slowed down to say hello.


      “Hello Leroy, when we gonna get some bales in?” daddy queried.


      “I suspect we might start cuttin’ next week sometime.” Grampa
answered.

      “Well when ever you need this boy of mine to get the work done he’ll
be more than happy to do it.” daddy gave me a playful shove in the shoulder
and grinned.

      “Well if that all we got helping us we better get started tomorrow.
How’s five o’clock sound to ya boy?” Grampa was teasing with me. He knew I
had three more weeks of school to go to.

      “In the morning? Five in the morning?” I whined.

      The two of them laughed a little over the low rumble of both the
trucks and tractors engines.

      “Let us know and we’ll be glad to help out, Leroy.” Daddy sounded
aloud as he let the clutch out and pointed the truck to the house. Grampa
waved and slowly pulled the tractor into the barn.

      The milk jug was sweating and was slippery as I pulled it out of the
trucks seat and jogged into the house. Daddy wiped sweat from his brow with
a red handkerchief and limped his way into the house. His knees were sore
from the drive home.
