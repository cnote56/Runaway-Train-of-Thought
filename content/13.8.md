The Harp

Few jobs in Xistence were as crucial as Dobly’s. He was the essential
Harp Keeper and was rarely seen outside. But there he was, Dobly,
walking down the street like he was not supposed to be back in the Harp
tending to the water.

The Harp is the most essential thing in Xistence. Only some places have
one, not even on Zist. Zist is known for having everything.

The Harp; a beautiful thing. A machine of wonderful metal, gifted to the
people from the gods and their palaces in the sky. Always obsessively
kept spotless, shiny, and clean. Dobly toiled tirelessly within the
gears and underbelly of the metal beast to keep it anchoring the
levators that took the offerings mined from the ground to the gods in
their flying palaces above.

The Harp. A series of rotating circles holding cups upward and filled
with oils and waters of varying degrees of heat and measures. In perfect
balance based on the other harps around this world but also in concert
with the stars aligned overhead.

Normally, his nostrils stung from the ionized gases and vapors of the
Harps fission elements. But today, he was in the sunshine and too busy
smelling the fresh air and watching a spaceship dock to the spaceport in
the sky to think about his obligations left neglected back inside the
Harp.

[Image]

Any simple energy source did not power the Harp. Nothing like fire or
heat could be as substantial as the Harp’s frequency intonations. The
oils and waters, in varying degrees, heat, and measures, all play into
the frequencies the harp wants to project to other harps, the stars, and
people that can hear it. Dobly was one of the select few that could
listen to these tones and frequencies, and from an early age, he was
pressed into the belly of the harp to tend its oils and waters in
varying degrees, heats, and measures. One oil balanced on a circle may
grow fat with extra moisture in the air, and its density increases. That
would cause the lighter waters on the other side of the same process to
rise because their density is much less than that of fat oils. This
caused the circle to rotate, which turns the axis gear holding the three
sister circles together in a beautiful and highly complex dance. The
other two sister circles, in turn, rotated because of the movement in
the main axis gear, and that caused the sister circle's oils and waters
in varying degrees, heats, and measures to move because the central axis
gear moved their circle. Sometimes this would offset the oils and waters
in varying degrees, heats, and steps, and this is where Dobly - or
someone like him - would be in the harp watching these shifts in the
oils and waters in varying degrees, heats, and measures. He would jump
to action and help calm that harp down and back to a restful gentle
rotation.

Once on a planet far away, a harp grew agitated, and its oils and
waters, in varying degrees, heat, and measures, revolted against the
central axis control and the sister circles holding them. The gears
ground and fought against the elemental pressures, but it was all lost
as the oils and waters in varying degrees, heat, and measures flung off
into the air. The harp spun wildly out of control, which turned the
frequency engines in the cores of that planet's internals and did
terrible things to the balance of life that called that planet home.

The frequency engines in the planet's center emit these forces we call
frequencies and pressures that move other things. Sometimes they affect
the oils and waters in varying degrees, heat, and measure themselves,
and the Harp Keeper would add a drop minus a drop, rotate a vase or
clean a lens. This maintenance kept the Harp clean and glorious and,
more importantly, rotating steadily and predictably.

If you knew the Harp Keeper was in the Harp, you knew the Harp was
functioning correctly.

But here we have Dobly, not keeping what he was charged to keep since
his ear could hear frequencies. The mouth agape and eye; he’s
heavenward, dumbfounded by the sight of a spaceship docking with the
spaceport.

This thing - the spaceship docking - happens all the time. Why is Dobly
so amazed by this one?

The rotation was growing faster at the Harp, which is not usually like
this. The fat oils were sloshing, and the waters to dropping. The
off-balance was increasing more significantly than the balance.

The central axis gears took the imbalance and twisted its main core
axle, and the gruel grew from below. A low unsettling growl began to
grow. It could be mistaken for the soft purr, but it grew agitated when
petting. The frequencies found their ears.

The flutterers whipped into a frenzy. Some poor things heard the
frequencies and thought up was down and down was up. Their dead little
feathered bodies were crushed on the ground in mere moments.

Some frequently sensitive guts turned sick, and lurchit projected from
their orals connected to those guts. Lighting storms triggered across
the waters of the bay under the setting sunset and the docked spaceship
that captured Dobly’s attention - which should have been focused back on
the oils and waters in varying degrees, heats, and measures that were
very kept at this violent moment.

The waters grew random as if they were the skin of a drum that had just
been struck with a billion drumsticks. The spiky pulse of the frequency
took the form of jagged rock and roll of its wavelengths.

Dobly’s eyes fixed on the docked spaceship. His fingers tapped on his
wrist.

Suddenly a jolt of frequency. Transports and people on the street jumped
and shrieked in fear. Not Dobly. He stood there oblivious, watching the
docked spaceship.

Then an explosion of energy erupted out of the water sending a shockwave
toward the docked spaceship in the sky. The energy raced toward the
spacecraft out of the atmosphere and soon found its target. The energy
blast rocked the spaceship and cracked it along its central axis.
Explosions sent pieces of the spacecraft floating into space and raining
down to the surface.

Dobly stopped tapping his wrist with his fingers and mouthing words with
his mouth. He turned on his heels and ran back to the Harp.

Maybe he suddenly woke up to the apocalypse around him.

Crashing through the door of the Harp Building, he raced to the cooling
controls; he couldn’t fight the weight of the oils. They were too strong
with momentum. Dobly had to cool the whole contraption down to get the
density back to similar before he could begin calculating which oil and
waters got drops given and taken to find a balance before the frequency
engines inside the planet tore it apart, similar to the other world
before.

Dobly’s mind was frantic with the math and music in his head that a Harp
Keeper had to master to control the harp and keep the world from
cracking.

He pulled levers and pressed keys at his control panels. The harp was
not responding. The cold blasts of air from the cooling vents barely
frosted the vases that held the oils and waters in varying degrees,
heats, and measures.

Heat.

The thought struck Dobly between his eyes like a hammer hit a nail. He
ordered up blasts of heat to increase the viscosity of the oils and to
humidify the waters. It might work.

The groan of the central axis lessened. The rotation slowed from the
main gear, and the sister circles slowed. The Harp was settling down.

Dobly wasted no time. He whirled toward a control panel and called up a
program to his screen. The keys clicked and clicked as he loaded a
program that, as it ran, looked like it was scanning blocks of data
fields in the harps controlling computers marking the data, and then
deleting the data in flashes of bright light - like fires to a cotton
field.

The door of the Harp Building pounded and shouts from outside exclaimed
for Dobly to answer the door and then answer their prosecutions.

Dobly looked back at his control screen and saw the data field ablaze
and darkened.

That’s enough. Dobly thought to himself. They’ll be fixing that for
ages. Long after, I’m forgotten. And Dobly reached into his vest pocket
and retrieved a small pill box. Cracking it open, a bright green pill
glowed in his eyes. He took the pill in his thumb and index fingers and
popped the glowing pill into his mouth.

Swallowing the pill as the door finally broke from the mob outside,
Dobly grinned and waved to all his old friends: the Maestir, the
Technologian, the Falmina, the Law-mun, and the Medician.

The light that we all have inside us was gone from Dobly’s eye before
his arm dropped to his side and his knees gave out, and he crumpled to
the floor.

The shouting and commotion flooded the room. The Medician rushed to
Dobly, but there was nothing to be done. He was dead - ponk foam and
spitle coated his mouth.

The Majestir and a Techlogian raced to control panels and then panicked
as they stumbled from from undiscernable screen to another. Frantic
commands and clacking of keys fired like the fully-automatic light
cannons trained on their civilsation by the Arched Metaliod Ships
hovering in the sky above.

Flamina checked the room for flashing lights and sparks, and he was
worried the axis was cracked and maybe the gears were twisted. The
friction and lock up might cause a meltdown of the pluntonim-laced
urnamital causing a meltdown at the atomic levels and decimating the
entire continent.

Lawman began taking notes on his notepad and photos with his photo pad.

Techlogican nodded the Harp was functioning correctly and balanced.

Medician looked up from Dobly’s corpse and said, “He’s dead, Jim.”

Jim, the Majestir, had known Dobly since before he was a student at the
university. He got to know Dobly because Jim always loved the science
behind the harp but needed help understanding the or could hear, for
that matter, the frequencies. To Jim, it was like reading sheet music
and hearing the music in your head but never hearing it with your ears
when you played the instrument.

“Why,” Jim asked aloud and to no one in particular.

Medician busied himself with covering Dobly’s body with a sheet.

Law-mun shrugged and continued his notetaking.

Techlogian was fevered with worry and terror at the screens glowing
infront of him.

Jim walked outside, back onto the street, and looked around at his city.

The damage was minimal.

No big fires or flooding.

There was a lull to the calamity. The cracks in the buildings were
small, nothing major to the structures. People were shaken, but they
were functioning. His eye glanced upwards then his head tilted back to
see the fire in the sky.

The frequencies had overloaded the levators and set loose an awful
collection of terrible events as his eyes traced the levators anchor
points to the ground, they were shattered. The ships above held in the
sky were cracked; some shattered and slowly coming apart in orbit.

Ambassador was on that ship! His thoughts shot almost out of his mind as
the horror unveiled above his impotent station. Ambassador was to meet
with the Lightning Bringer leaders to discuss a treaty regarding special
metals that could upgrade the harps. Jim had hoped for better working
conditions to also come from above, but that was now a far flung thought
cast into the deep oceans of his mind.

Jim glanced back through the open door of the Harp control building at
the sheet over Dobly’s body. He looked upward to see the burning
spaceships in the sky.

This was too random to be random. Jim thought to himself, and in his
mind the seeds of conspiracy took root.

Dobly had just done what only one man couldn’t have done.

The glowing pill.

The sabotage of the harp.

Dolby’s suicide.

The hell slowly decending from the sky above soon destroyed all of these
thoughts and no one else ever thought them again.

The Nacotchtank were an indigenous Algonquian people who lived in the
area of what is now Washington, D.C., during the 17th century¹. The
Nacotchtank village was within the modern borders of the District of
Columbia along the intersection of the Potomac and the Anacostia river¹.
The name Nacotchtank, which exists in several historical variants
including Nacostine, Anacostine, Anaquashtank, Nacothtant, Nachatanke,
is derived from the word “anaquashatanik”, which means "a town of
traders"¹.

The Nacotchtank were a trading people as they were established on
fertile land with the nearby rivers¹. In his 1608 expedition, English
explorer John Smith noted the prosperous Nacotchtank and their great
supply of various resources¹. The Nacotchtank spoke a language within
the Algonquian subfamily, a language group whose variants are spoken
among many tribes living along the coast of the Atlantic Ocean¹.
Specifically, the Nacotchtank spoke the Piscataway dialect as they were
closely associated with the larger Piscataway Chiefdom of Southern
Maryland, whose Tayac or grand chief ruled over a loose confederacy of
area tribes¹.

The Nacotchtank paid tribute to the Piscataway chief who resided to the
south in the nearby village of Moyaone (present-day Accokeek)¹. Various
pieces of art and other cultural artifacts such as hair combs, pendants,
and pottery, and dog bones have been found in excavations throughout
Washington, D.C., on Nacotchtank territory¹.

After the neighboring Province of Maryland began encroaching on
Nacotchtank territory in pursuit of land for tobacco plantations, the
Nacotchtank were forcibly removed¹. The Nacotchtank were last recorded
in the late 1600s as taking refuge on nearby Theodore Roosevelt Island
located in the Potomac River¹. Over time, the small population that was
left behind after battle and disease was absorbed by Maryland’s
Piscataway tribe¹.

be much higher ¹².

THE LONG WAY HOME; AFTER YEARS OF INDIFFERENCE AND EROSION, A GLAMOROUS
MANSION ON A GENTLE GEORGETOWN RISE FOUND ITS WAY TO AN OWNER WHO HAS
MADE THE PLACE HIS LIFE

Mention Halcyon House to anyone who's spent time in Georgetown and the
stories start to flow. A few who have toured the house recently, at the
end of an almost two-decade-long restoration, tell of the
color-saturated walls, the spectacular rooftop library and the
phenomenal underground sculpture studio. But more often tales from its
less pristine years come forth, accounts of ghosts that roam the
212-year-old structure, causing doors to slam and lights to flicker.
Stories of Albert Adsit Clemens, an addled owner who at the turn of the
century surrounded the original stately Georgian home with a Palladian
facade and two wings -- and then filled the mansion with trap doors,
stairways that led nowhere and myriad apartments and rooms, some only
large enough to hold a table and chair. There are those who remember its
years as a dormitory, when a contingent of demure young Georgetown
University ladies studied in the marble chapel and had tea in the
boxwood gardens. And there were many more years in which an eclectic
parade of people moved in and out of the main house and its surrounding
apartments, turning the crumbling red brick building overlooking Key
Bridge into part group house and part artist's atelier. In Georgetown,
it seems, everyone knows someone who has lived in that peculiar building
at 34th and Prospect. More recently, though, talk has centered on
45-year-old sculptor John Dreyfuss, who back in 1978 set out to "unravel
the knot," as he says -- to have this neglected, 21,000-square-foot
historic property returned to its glory days. The talk tends to revolve
around one theme: What is it about Halcyon House, named for the bird
with the power to charm winds, that has made one man spend 17 years --
more than half his adult life -- on a single massive and all-consuming
undertaking? And how on earth did he manage to actually live and produce
sculpture in the house, even as it was crumbling around him and slowly
sliding toward the Potomac? IT SHOULD COME as no surprise that Dreyfuss,
a fourth-generation Washingtonian, is a serious man, someone who chooses
his words with a certain degree of caution. A frivolous person would not
have agreed to take on a project of this length and breadth. He's the
kind of man who, when describing the history of Halcyon House, refers to
Benjamin Stoddert, a pallbearer at George Washington's funeral and the
man who built the house in 1783, as "Mr. Stoddert." Even Albert Adsit
Clemens, the eccentric owner said to have wandered around Georgetown
clad in torn clothes and an ancient hat, is respectfully called "Mr.
Clemens." (That's certainly more respect than Clemens's neighbors gave
him; they talked openly of him being "touched," a man convinced that as
long as he kept adding to the building he would never die.) Dreyfuss was
28 when he began living at Halcyon House in 1978, but his family's
connection to the property dates back to 1966, when Georgetown
University sold the mansion and grounds for $275,000 to a partnership
that included his father, Edmund Dreyfuss, the architect and developer.
His father already owned property just south of the house, along M
Street NW, and hoped to develop the area. "The house was acquired as a
business venture," says the younger Dreyfuss, sitting at the long sturdy
table in the handsome kitchen, in the part of the original structure he
shares with his wife, Mary Noble Ours, who is a portrait photographer.
"The hope was that the historic home would be part of a complex that
would include an entrance to the Metro for the west end of Georgetown.
When the Metro went across the river to Rosslyn, the partnership then
was left with what they would do with this historic property." The
partnership was also left with a bit of a controversy. All the talk in
the late '60s of bringing the Metro to Georgetown -- a heated dialogue
that involved the entire city -- had raised the profile of Halcyon
House. Until Edmund Dreyfuss and his partner, Blake Construction Co.,
bought the property, it had chugged along with little fanfare for more
than 180 years. "All of the abutting property owners were concerned
about how the building would evolve -- which is their right," says
Dreyfuss. "The people in Georgetown are no different than any other
community in the city -- they care about what goes on next to them.
There were legitimate grievances." Over the next 10 years, as the 16
apartments continued to be rented to an endless number of young
Georgetowners, the partnership came up with building plan after building
plan, none of which went anywhere. Because the property had been listed
in 1971 on the National Register of Historic Places, development plans
had to be vetted by a variety of local and federal agencies. Crucial to
all of the plans were the gardens that make up the south end of the 1
1/4-acre property. "The partnership tried very hard to alter its plans,
most of which had to do with the use of the garden," says Dreyfuss. "The
question always came down to this: What to do with this large garden?
Should it be developed or is it part of the historic structure of the
building? To make a very, very long story short, all the proposals that
the partnership made pertaining to the development of the historic
gardens met with serious, legitimate objections." In other words, by

Harvard's Graduate School of Design. From 1973 to 1978 he worked as an
apprentice in a series of sculpture studios in Connecticut. "I was asked
if I would move into a portion of the building to manage it while we
waited to see what would be the future of the building," he says.

leases expired. This would have to be the first step in reclaiming the
building, no matter what its future held. As he got enmeshed in Halcyon

and more of the original 18th-century building was taken up with various
renovations and restorations and changes," explains Dreyfuss, who during
the early years camped out in the 19th-century chapel, complete with
crypt and pulpit, that Clemens had built. "So really what it had become

all those changes. Things had reached the point where the 18th-century
structure was literally imploding." The crumbling state of the imposing
edifice wasn't going unnoticed by the neighbors. "Here was this stately
mansion that, from year to year, was simply deteriorating and
deteriorating," says Gill Ravenel, director of design at the National

first memories of Halcyon House actually date back to childhood visits
with his mother. "You had this feeling that it was going to sort of

Dreyfuss, thinking back to his days as a long-haired 28-year-old, "a
great portion of the building was covered with ivy, and there was a
tremendous amount of structural destabilization. Plaster was rotting,
the roof was falling apart and about 80 percent of the exterior wood was
rotted." An unfinished, window-filled attic space added by Clemens --
and one of the first areas Dreyfuss gutted, transforming it temporarily
into a sculpture studio -- was filled with 100 years of debris and

the rainwater that came through the roof," says Dreyfuss. "I don't think
even Miss Havisham would have lived here!" But as he worked on Halcyon
House, Dreyfuss began to discern within the decrepit jumble the outline
of the kinds of buildings in which he had lived when he was a sculptor's
apprentice. "While in Connecticut, I became extraordinarily interested
in and enamored with the beauty of 18th-century architecture," he notes.
"I think it's the closest art form to pure sculpture in architecture and
building. When I came back to Washington -- because I had been immersed
for so many years in that environment, I recognized almost immediately
that behind all the sleeping porches, in rooms that had been subdivided,
rooms whose ceilings had been dropped, floors which were sagging and
partitions that had been compromised -- I saw very clearly the
18th-century structure, and for me it really resonated." As Dreyfuss
proceeded to clear out the interior, he continued to try to interest
other development teams in the project.

"From 1978 to 1982, I brought some of the finest developers in our city
through here," he says. "They looked at it, came up with proposals, and
went before the Commission of Fine Arts for informal discussions. Each
of these teams was told that they would have to acquire the property and
come before the commission with a legitimate proposal and take their
risks." The Commission of Fine Arts, a federal agency charged with
guiding the planning and architectural growth of Washington, is not in
the position of assuring commercial interests of the viability of their
proposals, Dreyfuss explains: "In other words, the commission said, Put
up or shut up.' " One final offer was made in 1982 -- to give Halcyon

had launched the family on this odyssey, died of another stroke. At this
point in the saga, the 16-year-old partnership began to dissolve. That's
when the Dreyfuss family began talking about taking on the project

characteristic intensity, explaining that by family he means his mother,
Lorraine, his older brother, Ken, and a collection of aunts and uncles
from both sides of the family. "All of them knew that once we started
it, there would be no turning back. We needed a complete consensus of
every member of our family if we were going to take this on. Because
once you unravel the Gordian knot, you have a real beast on your hands."

breakdown, he is loath to talk about money -- as in, just how much did

Associates, that would come up with a restoration plan and then take

only say that restoring Halcyon House was "a private development, and a

says. "You take this on, you stay put, there's no changing of your mind,
there's no walking away." You can almost hear the collection of aunts,
uncles and other family members giving Edmund Dreyfuss's youngest son
stern words of direction. Dreyfuss remembers being bewildered by the
task ahead. "Everyone involved had to jump off a wall, with no
assurances. The financing had to be stretched out over many years. Our
strategy was that whatever we did we would do to the best of our
ability, so that if at any time we had to give up the project, we would
leave an armature in place that was the best armature we could leave."
In the end, the decade-long final push in the restoration of Halcyon
House brought the Dreyfusses closer together. "Ultimately, it wasn't a
matter of that we wanted to do it, but that we couldn't walk away from
it," he says. "And when you're involved in something that you can't walk
away from, it either tears you apart or binds you back together again.
The rebuilding of this house in the long run saved our family." BY THIS

think he was intellectually stimulated by considering all the options."

Key Person Insurance, also known as Key Man Insurance, is a life
insurance policy that a company purchases for the business owner or a
leading executive¹. If that person dies unexpectedly or becomes
disabled, Key Person Insurance provides compensation to keep the
business running until it replaces the executive or closes shop¹².

The “key person” named on the policy is someone who is considered
essential to running the business, whether it’s an owner/partner, top
executive, or someone with specialized knowledge and skills¹. Losing
this person would cause the business to suffer financially, as it would
be difficult and/or expensive to replace them, or because they bring in
a significant amount of revenue¹.

As for the history of Key Person Insurance, it’s hard to pinpoint a
specific origin, but it has been a vital part of business planning for
many years.

Let’s say a company, Company A, manufactures a unique type of widget.
The widget was invented by the company’s founder, who has unique skills
and knowledge critical to the production process. If the founder were to
pass away or become incapacitated suddenly, it could severely disrupt
the company’s operations, leading to financial losses.

To protect against this risk, Company A takes out a Key Person Insurance
policy on the founder. If an unfortunate event occurs, the insurance
policy would provide a payout to the company. This could be used to keep
the business running during the transition period, such as hiring and
training a replacement, and offsetting any lost revenue or increased
expenses.

It’s important to note that the policy's specifics, including the
coverage amount and terms, would be tailored to the company’s specific
needs and circumstances¹².
