# Chapter Two: The Poolesville Extraction

The bell rings. Not a jarring chime, but a clean, practiced pulse of sound. 

I don't move immediately. I look down at the ledger entry again—*I call it the harvest.* The ink is crisp, dated nearly sixty years ago, and yet it feels like an open order. I fold the paper, slipping it into the inner pocket of my coat. If the buyer is who I think he is, he doesn't want the first editions anymore. He wants to know if I’ve read the books.

I walk down the foyer, my steps measured. The Philadelphia house feels quiet—too quiet. It’s a museum of dead wealth, and I am the current caretaker of the decay. I open the door.

He’s wearing a suit that cost more than my first car, cut in a way that suggests he doesn't spend much time sitting in chairs. No frantic energy. No impatience. Just a calm, expectant stillness.

I open the door just enough to frame him. I don’t offer a smile.

"You're early," I say. My voice is flat. I don't give him the benefit of a question.

He tilts his head, a gesture of practiced empathy. "The market for these editions is volatile, Mr. Dooling. I find that punctuality is the only way to minimize the friction of acquisition."

He doesn't look at me; he looks past me, toward the study door at the top of the stairs. He’s scanning for the ledger. He knows exactly where it should be.

"Friction," I repeat. "Is that what we're calling it."

I step back, holding the door open wide. Not an invitation to enter, but an invitation to expose himself.

"The books are still in the study," I tell him. "Though, the ledger—the one that really matters—is taking me a bit longer to catalog. I’m finding the math in it quite… complex."

I watch his eyes. He’s a professional, but there’s a slight shift in his posture. A tightening of the mouth. He’s here for the harvest, and he realizes I’ve already started clearing the fields.

"Complex is good," he says, stepping over the threshold. "Complex is manageable. Simple is usually where the trouble lies."

I close the door behind him. The house feels smaller now. 

"Let's see what you've found then," he says.

***

I blink, letting the greed glaze over my eyes. I pull the ledger from my pocket, but I don’t hand it over. I slap it down onto the desk, right on top of a stack of real estate appraisals.

"Inheritance," I say, tapping the leather cover with a grin that’s a little too wide. "Right. Like a classic car or a watch. But you’re talking about companies. Shells. That sounds like a lawyer’s nightmare, not a retirement fund. I don't want a seat at a table that’s already burning down. I just want to sell the books, pay the taxes, and clear the Philly house."

I look at him like he’s a pest. "Look, if this ledger has real value—if it’s connected to the New York buyer—tell me. If not, I’m just going to toss it with the other estate junk. I’ve got an appraiser coming on Monday. If you want the ledger, make a bid. But don’t give me this 'scale of what he was running' lecture. I’m a grandson, not a partner."

I check my watch, looking pointedly toward the door. I’ve ignored his ultimatum. I’ve treated the "keys to the kingdom" like a stack of junk mail.

His face twitches. For the first time, he doesn't look polished. He looks panicked.

"You don't understand," he says, his voice dropping an octave, losing the smooth edge. "That ledger isn't just paper. It's the authorization key for the Q3 migration in the Sun-Belt. If that isn't logged into the system by Monday, the entire debt-to-equity conversion for the sector stalls. Do you have any idea what happens to the liquidity if the accounts aren't reconciled by Tuesday?"

I look at him, genuinely confused. "Liquidity? I’m talking about selling an estate. Are you buying the books or not?"

I turn my back on him, moving toward the window, peering out at the street. "You know, the appraiser mentioned that grandfather had ties to some pretty strange people. If you’re one of them, I think we’re done here."

He slams his hand onto the desk, rattling the lamp. He’s broken protocol. He’s revealed that the ledger is not just information—it’s a control device that they are currently chained to. 

He’s not just a buyer. He’s a middle-management cog in the BIC, and I just made him lose his place in the machine. He’s gone from hunter to exposed asset in the span of thirty seconds.

"You arrogant idiot," he hisses, his hand moving toward his jacket. "You have no idea what you’re holding."

I turn around, still acting the part of the annoyed heir. "Maybe. But I know when I’m being insulted. Get out. I’ll sell the ledger to the New York buyer. He seemed much less interested in my grandfather's 'legacy' and much more interested in the cash."

***

I pull my phone out, thumbing the screen with a practiced, casual sluggishness. I don't look at him, but I can feel the heat radiating off him.

"New York is already blowing up my messages about these 'assets,'" I lie, watching his reflection in the windowpane. I let a smug, impatient smirk touch my lips. "They seem to think this ledger is the 'Sun-Belt' key you’re so worried about. If that's true, why would I hand it to a guy who walks into my grandfather's study and starts making threats?"

I turn around, stepping into his personal space. I don't give him room to breathe. I make myself look like the spoiled, entitled heir who thinks the world is a playground—the very thing he’s been trained to despise and manipulate.

"You're sweating," I say, my voice low, dropping the high-pitched amateur act for a second. "That's bad for the upholstery. Look, if you want this—if it's as critical as you say—we need to talk somewhere that isn't under the eyes of the appraisers I've got coming. I know a place near the harbor. We discuss the real price there, or you walk out the front door and I hit 'send' on this email to the New York group."

I dangle the phone between us like a bait-hook.

"One hour. No backup. If I see a tail, the ledger goes into the fireplace, and you can explain to your 'Board' why their liquidity just vaporized because you lost your cool in a dead man's study."

He stiffens, his fingers curling into tight fists at his sides. He’s reaching for the bolt gun, but he’s terrified of the cost. He needs the ledger, and I’m the only one who can navigate the interface. I've forced him to negotiate with the one variable his machine couldn't account for: the grandson who truly doesn't care.

"One hour," he grinds out. "Don't be late, Dooling. You have no idea how much you're gambling with."

"Oh, I think I do," I reply, opening the study door for him. "That's why the stakes are so high."

***

The harbor is a graveyard of rusted industrial relics, the perfect setting for a BIC cleanup job. I see his car parked under a single, flickering streetlamp.

I keep the lead-lined sleeve tucked under my arm, the ledger inside acting as a silent, invisible anchor. My phone—which I’ve set to max volume—is in my other hand. As I approach the driver-side window, the interference from the ledger’s onion-transmitter starts to bleed through the phone’s speaker, a low-frequency, rhythmic grinding sound.

I don't try to silence it. I just walk.

As I get within three feet, the interference hits his car's electronics. The dashboard lights flicker and die. His earpiece emits a high-pitched, piercing whine that makes him physically recoil, clutching the side of his head. He looks at me, and his face goes pale as he realizes the sound is coming from me—or rather, from the bag I'm carrying.

I tap on the glass, twice. *Thump. Thump.*

He rolls the window down, his composure shattered by the electronic screeching filling the cabin. "What the hell is that?" he gasps.

I lean down, resting my arms on the frame of the window, smiling like I’m just an idiot who doesn’t understand technology. 

"Sorry about that," I say, my voice light, casual. "I think the books are making the phone act up. Grandfather always said these antique bindings were 'charged,' but I figured he was just being poetic."

I reach into the lead sleeve, and I don't pull out the ledger. I pull out my phone, which is now outputting a steady, guttural white noise. I hold it right near his dash. His onboard computer starts cycling through errors, the screens flashing red: SYSTEM FAILURE. CRITICAL DATA LOSS.

"You said this ledger was a key," I whisper, my tone suddenly dropping all the feigned ignorance. "But it feels more like a bomb. You want to talk about the Sun-Belt? Or do you want to tell me exactly which node in the BIC is going to lose their head when they realize you’ve turned this entire harbor into a dead zone?"

He looks at the ledger in the bag, then at my phone, his eyes wide with the realization that I’ve neutralized his comms. He’s deaf to the Board. He’s alone in the graveyard, and he's talking to the grandson who isn't playing by the rules.

***

The harbor’s emergency foam system triggers, blinding the pier in chemical snow. As the operative flees, I am left alone in the freezing fog, my phone blinking with "ACCESS GRANTED." The Auditor has seized control of the Sun-Belt nodes. 

I reach the Aviary—Site-9—near Poolesville. It is a cavernous, subterranean hangar, a ghost of the Cold War. I climb into the mothballed interceptor, the Auditor’s voice now perfectly mimicking my grandfather’s cold, sharp cadence.

"You're going to Cuba," it tells me. "The Protectorate is waiting."

I slam the throttles forward. The interceptor leaps into the dark, rain-slicked night, carving a path toward the Atlantic. I leave the BIC cleanup team behind. I am flying into the heart of the machine I just broke.

I leave the briefcase behind, unopened, keyed to the Protectorate’s frequency. 

I am officially in the air.
