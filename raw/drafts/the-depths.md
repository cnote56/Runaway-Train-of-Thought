                                 The Depths




Light filled the room from a closed window. Lance Corporal Charles Morrison
sat in a wheelchair with a small wooden table in front of him. When I
entered the room his eyes just stared blankly into the air in front of his
nose he didn’t move. The hard soles of my shoes tapped on the yellow tiled
floor as I walked towards him.
      His name had been printed in every major paper of every city back
home. Some of them praised him others focused on how the horrors of this
war have affected him. None of them had talked to him, yet. They only
gleamed what they wanted to print from press releases I had prepared about
him.
      He was the sole survivor of an ambush, which happened a week ago. We
thought he was dead his whole company had been slaughtered by forces we
still haven’t been able to find.
      He looked like he had just lived through a title fight staged in a
slaughterhouse. He had bandages covering well over half of his body. His
shoulder was the worst part. It had been dislocated for so long the tendons
were all torn. His left wrist was shattered. One side of his rib cage had
been hit so hard every rib was broken all the way from his underarm to his
hip.
      “Corporal Morrison, my name is Lieutenant Darryl Richards. I’m here to
debrief you about…”
      “They came up through the streets,” Charlie’s hand shook as it made a
nervous wiping motion on his pant leg, “They seemed to pour out of the
houses…the alleyways,” he paused and looked into my eyes, “Like water and
smoke they came out of everywhere.”
      I quickly sat my briefcase next to the table and pulled my yellow
note pad out as he spoke. His eagerness to talk to me was awkward; I
thought he wouldn’t want to tell me anything.
      “They had children in front of them. They were pushing their crying
faces and scared little bodies ahead of them while they shot their AK-
47’s!” His hand rushed to cover his face.
      “Listen to me, Charlie, calm down, take a deep breath remember you’re
out of all that now. You want something to drink?” I motioned to the coffee
pot on a table across the room, “Let me hear it.”
      On the wooden table between us I laid a pack of cigarettes and my
tape recorder and pressed a button making it a mute witness to this
debriefing. My hand searched my briefcase for a pen.
      “God, I missed that,” He gestured to the sunlight warming the floor,
table and walls.
      “Tell me about it,” I asked him as I found my pen, “I know we found
you in a sewer ditch. How did you get there?”
      “I crawled. Seems like I crawled through half this goddamn country on
my belly through filth,” he sighed, “Through stuff you couldn’t begin to
imagine.”
      “How did you get into the sewers?”
      “Get in? That’s where they had been keeping us. They kept us locked
up in the darkness. Deep underground, in what seemed like miles and miles
of tunnels. They kept us chained to the damn walls like animals.”
      “Okay, but how did you escape?”
      The ring on my finger caught his eye.
      “College, Huh? I could have gone there. My dad wanted me to get my
education before I joined up. But two things stood in the way of that
dream. I didn’t want to go to college, and I wanted to fight. Get some time
in for my country. Serve my homeland. Give back for all the things all the
useful idiots out there took for granted.”
      “Useful Idiots?” My interest was peaked.
      “Yeah, Lenin wrote about the Useful Idiots of a society creating a
fifth column inside it by which an opposing force would attack on all four
sides thus leading themselves to their own demise and benefiting the outer
attacking forces.”
      “You’ve read Lenin.” There was a sense of shock in my voice.
      “Yeah, a dumb little jarhead like me, who’s mother was terrified her
blue eyed baby boy was gonna get him self killed over all this, has read
Lenin. Just cause I didn’t go to college doesn’t mean I can’t read. She was
terrified by the thought, cause I’m her only son.”
      His pause let me catch up on notes I was scribbling down on a legal
pad. The sun felt warm at my back and it was only eight o’clock in the
morning. Here in the desert the sun doesn’t care what time it is. In the
desert the sun is always hot and sand is everywhere. Little grains of sand,
like yellow diamonds, have a way of cutting into everything they touch.
I’ve seen the inside of M-16 become completely frozen by just a few grains
of these little yellow diamonds. But it’s been at least a year since I have
seen an assault rifle much less heard it fired in anger. I had been stuffed
into a back room at headquarters even since I had been sent to this sand
box.
      His bandaged hand reached slowly forward for the pack of cigarettes
next to the tape recorder. He jerked the pack upwards with one hand to get
a cigarette to pop up. His face grimaced with pain from his heavily
bandaged left shoulder. His hand shook a little as he tossed the pack back
on the table. I reached into my left chest pocket and withdrew my lighter
to light his cigarette. My chair creaked a little with my weight rising off
of it as I reached over the narrow table to flick the lid open, struck at
the flint and lit this mans cigarette.
      “We don’t have enough men out here. You know right. Logistically we
don’t have the footprint for this stuff. We’re also taking all this
military occupation with way too many diplomatic stances,” his words were
true and there was nothing I could say to either confirm or deny my opinion
on the matter. I was here to report on what happened not what will or
should happen.
      The tobacco burned at edge of the white cigarette. The cherry colored
ember glowed as he sucked in a heavy breath. I placed my lighter on top of
the cigarettes.
      “After World War II, we imposed moratoriums and martial law. Any
weapon over three inches long was confiscated or turned in. Anybody who
didn’t conform was rounded up, prosecuted by military law, and punished for
their actions. Punishment was usually shooting or hanging. But with twenty-
four hour news, we aren’t gonna do that. Heaven forbid we do what needs to
be done. Heaven forbid.”
      His raised his clenched hand with the cigarette up in front of his
face like a torch and with smoke curling out of his mouth and nose said,
”You know these things will kill ya. Guess the same thing could be said
about what happened to me.”
      I could see tears welling up in his eyes not from the smoke in his
lungs and face, but from what I can only guess was his reflection on what
had happened over the past week.
      “Listen, Charlie, you’re a hero back home. Your gonna get a parade
when…”
      He interrupted me with an icy stare. “A hero? For what, crawling
through shit and filth? For surviving and sacrifice? For living through
something Dante couldn’t even envision? I’m a hero. Shit, if it were only
simple.”
      “Then tell me about it. Listen, soldier, regardless of what you think
about it the papers all read ‘POW Survives Torture.’ Do you hear that?
Survives…Charlie you survived. You are the best thing to happen to this war
effort since we took out one of his generals and I need to hear your story.
Then you get to get out of here and go home.” I meant my words to reinforce
him. I wanted him to feel proud of his survival.
      “I’m just a stupid grunt. All I know is how to shoot the bastard
shooting at me,” he inhaled a heavy breath from the cigarette.
      “What do I know about winning a war?” blue gray smoke plumed out of
his nostrils as he spoke. “When I get shot at…I shoot back. When I get
threatened,” he leaned closer to me, “I act. I’ve been trained to do it.
      “I joined up for it. I know what my job is for. I kill the bad guys
and protect the innocent. These people don’t like to think along the lines
of good and bad. Things are either shameful or honorable. They have grown
up in a shame-based society versus our guilt-based society.”
      The ash on his cigarette grew long at the tip.
      “You see their actions are based on wither their name or family will
be shamed by the action.” He flicked the ash into the ashtray next the tape
recorder and continued, “Our society is guilt based. Where the person is
socialized to believe bad action is still bad even if nobody ever finds
out.”
      He took a long breath through the cigarette closed his eyes, leaned
his head back, and opened his mouth. Smoke plumed out of his open mouth
like the exhaust of a smokestack. Somehow I knew this mans words were not
just coming from his experiences, but also had a lot to do with what this
man had boiling in his head as well as why he was here today—a survivor and
not a statistic.
      Tears welled up in his face as he began to speak:
      “I broke my wrist getting out of the chains they had me tied up with.
Tell that to the U.N. and their human rights follow the bouncing ball sing
along. This isn’t about politics, but the papers and news channels want to
make it that way. But it’s blood mixing in the streets with the filth of
these people. It’s lives on the line out there. Life or death, right and
wrong not contracts about oil or political posturing on either the national
or international scene. Don’t talk to me about just or unjust actions.
Don’t talk to me about right or wrong when my all my buddies gone.”
      He reached up to tap more ash off the tip of his cigarette. The tape
recorder hummed and my hands were numb from the mess of scribbles on my
yellow note pad.
      “Morrison, we’re not here talking about that. We need to deal with
what happened to you. What you went through,” I didn’t know if I was saying
it for his benefit or mine.
      He took a long pause and deep breath.
      “You know your body just goes numb after a while. I didn’t feel it. I
didn’t feel any of it after the first few times they beat us.
      “The bastards left us hanging there for days. I ran out of spit if you
know what I mean,” He chuckled to himself and continued, “ I didn’t tell
them a goddamn thing. Not a damn word. In fact, I started singing in German
just to throw them off. Hell, I threw in a few cuss words when I could.”
      “I didn’t know you spoke German. That wasn’t in your file,“ I
interrupted.
      “Good! Its not supposed to be. I didn’t want a desk job. I didn’t want
to be in the rear with the gear. That’s why I put in for the infantry, “ He
stated, “I wanted to fight.”
      “What kind of questions did they ask you?” I asked sensing he was
becoming more resolved with the memory of his strength returning to him.
      “All sorts, everything from my name, rank, final objective, to the
name of my dog back home. Told them to go screw themselves,” He began to
laugh nervously as his eyes locked on the corner of the table.
      A long pause hung frozen in the air between us. I waited uneasily for
him to begin again. Then I spoke softly, “Charlie. Buddy, you ok?”
      “I wasn’t the only one taken as a POW,” his confession sounded loudly
in my ears as shock washed over me. I was speechless.
      “Jack Dickson, Fred Pauletti, Henry Smith and Joe Stein.”
      He pulled a deep breath of smoke into his lungs. The sound of the
tobacco burning sounded like background music.
      Then my hand began to feverishly scribble down the names I just heard.
      “We were taken prisoner cause we were the last ones left alive. They
shot the officers first,” His eyes glanced at my college ring on my finger
and then to the insignia on my lapel, “Snipers usually do that. We ducked
between two tanks that had their tracks blow off with RPG’s. We ran out of
ammo so fast. I don’t know how long we were fighting but it felt like an
eternity. It was like Dante forgot to include a chapter; a chapter all five
of us lived,” he paused and sighed, “Henry was shot in the neck, I was
holding a bandage to the wound when we got over run.”
      The cigarette smoked itself at the end of his fingers as he dove
deeper into his experiences, ”They were screaming their God awful language
and pointing their bayoneted rifles at us. Jack was the first to stand up.
When he raised his arms above his head he looked more like a bony skeleton
in fatigues and a Kevlar jacket than a Marine. His skinny arms looked so
out of place with all the equipment we all had to wear. As he pushed my
hand away from his wounded throat Henry gurgled. I think he mumbled
something like, ‘I got it.’  I froze.
      “My muscles wouldn’t respond. Stein was the strongest of us all. I
think it was cause he had the most to lose out of all of us. You see, Henry
came from the south his dad was in the marines and he wanted to keep up the
tradition. Jack and Fred joined up together straight out of High School.
The both of them wanted to get into college so they joined the military for
the experience as well as the money,” He paused to take a long drag from
his cigarette.
      His hesitation made me think about his relationship with Stein, there
was something deeper there, “Stein was a career man. He joined up straight
out of high school. God he was smart. He knew just about anything you could
throw at him. Him and me would debate constantly in the barracks.
      “Everything. Every little issue I could think of. Anything from the
newspapers to what the talking heads shouted over the radio. He also had a
wife and two kids. He would invite me over on the weekends when I was off
duty to have barbeques and watch football games.” He paused and his lower
lip quivered, “He was the reason I reenlisted for all of this.”
      His hand trembled a little as it made a nervous twitch, which looked
like he was cocking a gun all the while the cigarette held a delicate
balance in his pale hand.
      His mouth opened slowly and spoke, “Stein was like a brother to me,
but he was also Jewish.” His arm rose with some amount of pain pulsing from
his shoulder to tap the cigarette into the ashtray.
       “What happened then, Charlie?” I felt some amount of childlike
intrigue with my speech but also I knew I had to find out the truth of what
this man had gone through.
      “They lined us up against the wall cocked their guns and took aim. But
an officer shouted something making them stand down. He walked over to us.
He didn’t even step over the bodies. Looked like he was walking in a half
dry riverbed and trying not to get his feet wet.  The bastard stepped right
on the torn open bellies and wounds just to get a little blood on those
shiny black boots. He shouted a phrase I could never understand but will
never forget. The soldiers tied us at the neck to each other and lead us
into an alleyway like we were cattle.
      “I watched as they took boots, guns, ammo, knives, helmets anything
they could strip from the dead in the street. Left them there to rot like a
calling card. My eyes locked on the eyes of the officer who stopped the
execution and I…” He stopped.
      “Charlie? You okay? You need a minute?” We were getting close to
something but the only question was did I want to get that close.
      “I saw his dark eyes. Didn’t know the devil could walk like a man,” he
sniffed as his nose ran a little and wiped his face with the back of his
good hand. “You could set a clock by the frequency they beat us at. It
wasn’t interrogation. Brutality doesn’t even come close. Freddy was the
first to go. He didn’t crack. He didn’t say a goddamn thing,” Charlie’s
tears ached out of his eyes, “ Every time they clocked him, he’d spit right
in their face. Blood, spit…teeth.”
      “Charlie, do you need to stop?”
      “Teeth, bits of teeth, whole molars. Do you hear what I’m saying? None
of us talked.  We suffered in hell for all that time and suddenly I see
those pigs marching in our cities without knowing what I…We had to live
through,” His voice was heavy with anger.
      My pen dropped to my legal pad and I reached for a cigarette and the
lighter in one motion.
      “I’m sorry for all of that. You need the truth not my opinion,” he
sighed as a tear rolled down his cheek.
      “Its okay. Charlie, you needed to get it off your chest. I’m here to
get the info from you. Let you give me the story.”
      “Okay, well they didn’t feed us, not a thing. They kept us in a room
with hoods over our heads. The chains they had us tied up with cut off the
circulation to my fingers. My right hand is still a little numb but just in
my pinky finger. Sometimes when the sessions would begin they would hang us
by our wrists from the center of the room. They would hang us on a hoop in
the middle of the room by the chains they never took off and beat us where
the rest of us could not help but witness. I don’t know why but they never
separated us. I guess they didn’t have the room down there in the pits.”
      My mouth was dry when my voice crept out of my throat, “Well…Tell me
about he place they kept you. Do you know where it was?”
      “Well, it was dark most of the time. The only time they would turn on
a light was when they would come to beat us. Or when the guard shined a
flashlight on us as he walked his rounds.
      “That place was part sewer…part catacomb…part hellhole to us. I could
here water trickling constantly. The place seemed circular like it was
built in between the streets as they spread out in their different
directions, like a web under the city. I can’t be sure but they never took
us more than a mile away from where they captured us.”
      “How did you escape?” The question shot out of my mouth even before I
could think it.
      “Well they left us chained for the whole time. I…I don’t know how many
days. I was beaten and bruised beyond belief.” He sighed and exhausted
breath, ”But I was in the best condition of all of them. Stein ordered me
to get out of my chains. That’s were I did this,” He raised his bandaged
forearm and left hand.
      “He said, ‘Morrison, take our tags and you run. You get outta here and
get this intel back to the real world. I’d go with you, but I think they
hit me once too many in the back. I’ve been trying to move these legs of
mine for two days and they won’t budge.’ So I had to leave them there. Jack
hung there, by his wrists, he died a few minutes after the last session.”
      My pen scribbled feverously on my yellow legal pad.
      “Henry hadn’t moved for a long time. His legs hung below him twisted
and bent in horrible angles legs shouldn’t ever be put in.”
      Morrison shifted nervously and painful in his chair and continued,
”Freddy couldn’t move his neck…his head…his head hung in a strange angle
from his shoulders. He hadn’t spoken to us for a long time. I was sure he
was dead. I could hear the guard’s footsteps sounding from down the
corridor. His flashlight danced its way in the darkness. Stein looked at me
and said, ‘Kill the pig.’”
      “And you did,” I interjected.
      “Yeah…snapped his neck like a twig just like they taught me in basic.
I waited until he got real close and reached out with my good arm and
caught his chin in between my bicep and deltoid. All I had to do was twist
and jerk real quick.
      “It felt strange almost surreal to see his body drop. I pulled the
body into our corner. I retrieved the tags from their necks as Stein told
me too. They didn’t have a cage door, that’s why they used the chains, I
guess. Stein looked at me just before I left and…”
      He stopped abruptly.
      “What? What happened next Morrison?” I was hanging at the edge of my
seat.
      “Nothing. I took one last look at him then found a drain and dove into
it.” He said nervously and reached for another cigarette, like he was
trying to keep his throat from shouting at me some terrible fact. He looked
away as I saw a tear drip from his cheek.
      “Then the crawling and the drainage ditch,” I suggested.
      “Yeah…Yeah, that’s it,” he pulled hard on the cigarette and continued
his avoidance of my stare. “I remember the brightness I crawled to. At
first it was a small bright white circle far away from me and slowly it got
bigger until I was free and bathed in the glow of the sun. When I saw the
army green towering over me—it was beautiful. Nothing could make me stop
crying I was so happy to be out of that hell hole.”
      “And now you’re here waiting for me to let you go and get and back
home,” I added.
      “Yeah, I mean, Yes Sir!” His posture seemed to improve, as the
realization he was almost finished with his confession sunk in.
      “Well, ok let me just review all my information.” I flipped through
the yellow sheets of my note pad. “Captured two weeks in captivity-tortured
beyond description-escaped-crawled through he bowels of civilization and
then spat out into the hands of mother green.
      “A broken wrist, leg, dislocated shoulder, cuts, bruises and nerve
damage. Anything else, Marine?” I stated.
      His service had earned him more than what I could ever write in my
report but I know he earned the title of Marine, at least.
      “Uh…no…no, Sir! That’s about all of it, Sir.”
      “Well, okay Private. Thank you, Son. Thank you, Marine.” I said as I
stuffed papers and a pen into my briefcase.
      His eyes were nervously shifting from my briefcase to my face as I
prepared to leave. Beads of sweat had formed on his brow.
      “Wait,” His words sounded with a strange soberness to my ears, ”Sir,
there is something else.”
      I thought to myself what on earth could be left to say as I settled
back into my chair leaving my legal pad and pen hanging half way out of my
briefcase.
      “The dog tags. The ones around my neck,” his head lowered and tears
began to flow out of his eyes.
      “What dog tags?” I thought to myself.
      He reached forward and clicked off the tape recorder. And in silence
his head raised to show me his tear filled eyes.
      “The dog tags I ended up with, Sir,” his voice shaking slightly like
he was trying to spit something out that was choking him, “I said I removed
them from Dickson, Pauletti, Smith and Stein just before I left but that’s
not all. Dickson, Pauletti, and Smith were all cold to the touch when I
took their tags.
      “But…But Stein looked me square in the eye as I removed his from his
neck. He said, ‘Morrison, do me a favor. When you survive and get back
stateside get those to my wife. You tell her they didn’t get the best of
this Jew. You tell her I died fighting. Spitting in their eyes.’ ”
      Morrison’s face was soaked with tears and only good hand shook with
some horrible unknown guilt.
      “What happened next, Morrison? What else did Stein tell you?” I feared
the answer.
      “He…He said, ‘Now do it. Don’t let these rat bastards get the thrill
of getting themselves a notch on their belts with my Jew blood on their
hands. Don’t give them the pleasure!’ he then motioned to the side arm on
the dead body of the guard and nodded to me. ‘Do it Marine, that’s and
order!’ were his words to me” Morrison lunged forward and clutched my hand
just inches from the mute tape recorder. His tortured eyes locked on to
mine, his ribs and shoulder must have been screaming with pain. But there
was no grimace of pain in his face only a pleading mournful stare locking
our eyes together.
      “I followed my orders, Sir. I did like I was told to do. I did what my
CO told me to do. I followed my orders,” His words sounded like he was
trying desperately to convince both himself and me.
      “You…?” I began only to be interrupted.
      “I did what I was ordered to do. I did what Stein wanted me to do.”
      His sweat-smeared hand released mine and his body retreated back into
his wooden chair. His sobbing was now uncontrollable by any means.
      I struggled to find my voice. Trying to find the strength to speak. We
sat there not daring to find each other’s eyes.
      I broke the icy silence, “You followed orders, Marine. You followed
orders.” In my head I wondered did I repeat those words for his benefit or
mine.
      “Listen Morrison you took the tags, put them round your neck and
crawled belly down through hell. You followed orders, Son,” I leaned
forward grabbed the dead tape recorder, tossed it into the open mouth of my
briefcase and stood up. The wooden chair moaned as it slipped from the back
of my knees and pushed away from my seat. My hand clasped the handle of my
brown leather briefcase as I stepped around the table. Morrison began to
control himself as he choked back his tears.
      I placed my hand on his left shoulder, his undamaged one, and said,
“Son, you’re a fine Marine and you re one hell of a man.”
      His hand reached up crossing his heart to hold on to mine, which lay
cemented to his shoulder. The clock on the wall read one minute to high
noon and the red arm dictating the seconds slowly clicked down.
      “Without men like you this world would be a far worse place than we
could ever know. Without marines like you, Morrison,” I felt tears welling
up in my eyes, “we would be losing this war.”
      His head leaned downward and tilted at an awkward angle as my words
sunk into his mind. A sigh escaped his lungs as I saw another tear roll
down his cheek.
      Light continued to glow in through the window, as if the world outside
was engulfed in flames.
