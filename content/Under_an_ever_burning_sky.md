title: Under an ever burning sky

Under an ever burning sky.

The apple pie tumulted end over end splashing the golden pie crust over
the pie’s brains of slices and syrup.

He was 46 years old now, but he was still thinking about the apple pie
he dropped when he was seven. His mother yelled at him. He doesn’t
remember the yelling, but he remembers the broken pie along the sidewalk
toward the meeting house on a Sunday morning near Thanksgiving but he
was seen - what’s a calendar?

He didn’t remember the scolding but he repeated to himself nearly
everytime he felt small. Everytime e felt weak. Everytime he felt like
the loser he saw when the mirror found his gaze.

I can’t trust you to do a DAMN thing right, JOHNATHAN!

It was another dark moment for Jjon Echols. He had had the worst, but
tonight was still an awful night for himself.

CAN’T HOLD A DAMN PIE! CAN’T HOLD A DAMN JOB!!

Jjon was unemployed. But his mother didn’t know that and she wouldn’t
have known that when she stood over him screaming him smaller and
smaller. Even then she hollowed though his mouth at himself. Like he was
seven years old, in a 46-year olds basement and all equally terrified of
what the next day brings.

###

Email pinged his cellphone. His thigh singed from the buzz and tickled
by the radio bursts running in and out of him. He thought it. It doesn’t
mean it was real. But for a moment when it happened - he felt it. Not
the buzz, but the signal. The inperceviable signal from a radio tower
traveling in megahertz’s wrapped around raditiclum of the ionosphere and
everything in between. Jjon was at least sensitive to electronic
signals - he was sure of it.

The x-rays he took after football games he didn’t finish. He swore he
could feel

The ray shoot through him and the broken foot it was aimed at.

The MRI machine’s nexus of what felt like being wrapped in electrified
steel wool.

But back to his thigh and the phone pressed against it in his pant
pocket.

Now in his hand the screen wiped to the email.

“We have chosen to proceed with another candidate.”

Today would be another bad day for Jjon. The rejection hurts but a
person is supposed to “move on” from it and find the lemonade from the
lemons. Mixed metaphors never worked for Jjon.

Six years Jjon has wandered. For six months he’s been trying to find a
job. He’s been having emails like this for a while now. Not just one of
them, but all of them - he feels all of them everytime one hits… all
over again. And the next week’s unemployment hangs over him. Another
job. Another rejection. And another miserable day pounding himself with
self-doubt and loathing.

He had struggled with these feelings most of his life. Up to then he was
able to bounce an droll into the next swing. But Jjon had lost his step
and stumbled, fell down, and lost his way.

He was 8 years old. The mist had just lifted from the trees surrounding
a log cabin house belonging to his grandmother. The gravel road led up
and around the hill the cabin was built into. it was at least a quarter
mile long. At the end was to be a yellow bus that would be there at 6:15
am.

“Get your belt. Pull your pants up. And get on that bus, boy!” Jon’s
mother shouted at him from the darkness of the basement behind a wall of
moving boxes that had been in the back of large RYDER truck they had
unloaded after the last leg of a long one-way trip from the brown desert
where he was born and grew up now to green pine tree peppered place
where it was cold in the mornings.

It was 6:13 and Jjon was crying as his small gait kicked at the rocks
under them.

The bus rumbled from the highway toward the county road where the gravel
road Jjon was moping along.

Suddenly what little math Jjon’s eight-year old Brian could comprehend
did not look good for him to reach the bus before it passed the gate.

He kicked his feet into a run as the burden of the backpack slapped at
his low back.

Jjon was not going to make the bus. He was screaming and shouting for
the bus to stop, but it did not. It drove by leaving a rush and dust and
wind as it wake. Jjon missed the bus his mother had just screamed him
out the door to get to.

Jjons legs gave out his fatness pimpled down over his crossed legs in
the middle of the gravel road. His tears streaked at his cheeks. his
cries rented his throat. He feared going back there.

And as the ropes of his dread clung at his throat, the screech of bus
brakes and the creak-slump of the folding door.

“You’re the new kid, right? Dunch Farm. Yeah! I see you here on my
list!” The driver shouted at the crumpled boy sitting in the driveway.

Come on, kid! We gotta get to school!”

Jjon scrambled toward the bus door swiping his cheeks and trying not to
look like he was crying in front of the other school children and the
high school kids sitting in the back.

The cold green faux-pleather vinyl of the bus seat stuck to Jjons hands
as he tried to watch the trees and telephone poles washing by the
roadside as the bus barreled toward a school another hour and half away
from the 8:30 first bell.
